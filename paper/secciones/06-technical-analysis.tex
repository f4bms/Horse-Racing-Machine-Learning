\section{Technical Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

This section reports the exploratory analysis performed before any model
was trained. It is organised around one question: which information was
genuinely available when the starting gate opened, and which was not.
Feature admissibility, imputation and partitioning all follow from that
question. The analysis is reproducible from \texttt{scripts/eda\_hkjc.py}
with a fixed seed; every number below is regenerated by that script.

\subsection{Data Dictionary}
\label{sec:dict}

The dataset consists of two relational files published by
Daley~\cite{ejeC_daley_hkracing}, derived from the public race records of
the Hong Kong Jockey Club (HKJC). \texttt{races.csv} holds one row per
race (6\,349 rows, 37 columns) and \texttt{runs.csv} one row per
horse--race pair (79\,447 rows, 37 columns); the two join on
\texttt{race\_id}. The window spans 2 June 1997 to 28 August 2005 and
covers 4\,405 horses, 186 jockeys and 176 trainers at the Sha Tin and
Happy Valley courses. One race in \texttt{races.csv} has no corresponding
runners and is dropped, leaving 6\,348 usable races.

Table~\ref{tab:dict} summarises both files. Each field carries an
\emph{availability} label --- PRE for information published before the
race, MKT for market prices, POST for anything that only exists once the
race is decided. That label, not predictive power, determines
admissibility. The full dictionary with dtypes, cardinality, ranges and
null counts per column is generated into \texttt{reports/}.

\subsection{Data Leakage Audit}
\label{sec:leakage}

Predicting a race from a table that also contains the outcome is trivial
and worthless, so the admissibility rule was fixed before any feature was
written: \emph{a variable may enter the model only if its value was
determined and published before the gates opened for that race}. Applying
it column by column across both files discards 48 fields
(Table~\ref{tab:leakage}), leaving 13 raw pre-race attributes on the
runner table and 11 on the race table as the basis for feature
engineering.

The target \texttt{won} is a deterministic function of \texttt{result}
($\texttt{won}=\mathbf{1}[\texttt{result}=1]$), so keeping \texttt{result}
would amount to handing the model its label. The sectional blocks describe
the race as it unfolds. The payout fields deserve separate mention as the
most dangerous leakage here: they sit on the \emph{race} table, so a naive
join propagates them silently to every runner, and
\texttt{win\_combination} is literally the saddlecloth number of the
winner. They are dropped at load time, before any merge.

\texttt{win\_odds} and \texttt{place\_odds} are the \emph{final} tote
odds. They are not target leakage in the strict sense --- the price is
fixed at post time --- but they are excluded from the feature set for
three reasons. The closing price aggregates every bettor's information,
including late money, so a model built on it largely re-learns the crowd.
The resulting system would be useless in deployment, where a prediction is
wanted before the market settles. And mixing the market price with a
fundamental signal makes our research question unanswerable, since it asks
whether pre-race attributes alone carry information. Following the
two-stage practice of the racetrack efficiency
literature~\cite{ejeA_benter2008}, the odds are retained only as an
\emph{independent market baseline}.

Most of the signal lives in historical form rather than in the raw row: a
horse's prior win rate, jockey and trainer strike rates, days since the
last start, class movement. Every such feature uses an expanding window
over races strictly earlier in time. The frame is sorted by (entity,
\texttt{date}) and the aggregate taken as \texttt{shift(1).expanding()}
within the group, so a race never contributes to its own feature; no
statistic is computed over the full table. Debutants make this explicit:
5.5\,\% of rows have no prior start, so these features are null by
construction and receive a ``no history'' indicator rather than an imputed
mean, which would assign an average record to an unraced horse.

The split is temporal \emph{and} grouped by \texttt{race\_id}. A random
split would place runners of the same race on both sides, letting the
model exploit the one-winner-per-race constraint, and would let future
meetings inform past ones. Cut points sit at the 70th and 85th percentiles
of the date distribution: training through 16 February 2003
(55\,658 runners), validation through 20 June 2004 (11\,878), test
thereafter (11\,911). The script asserts that no race spans more than one
split, and the count is zero. Model selection uses forward chaining, never
shuffled $k$-fold. Imputers, encoders and scalers are fitted inside a
\texttt{scikit-learn} \texttt{Pipeline} on training folds only; where
high-cardinality identifiers are target-encoded, the encoding is computed
out-of-fold with prior smoothing, since a naive target encoding fitted on
the whole training set is itself leakage.

\subsection{Data Quality and Imputation}
\label{sec:quality}

Missingness arises from four distinct mechanisms, and collapsing them into
one imputation rule would be a mistake.

\emph{Structural absence.} The later sectional columns are null for short
races, which simply have fewer timing sections: \texttt{position\_sec4},
\texttt{behind\_sec4} and \texttt{time4} are missing in 33\,221 rows,
rising to 78\,151 for the sixth section. These are not missing values ---
the quantity does not exist --- and imputing them would fabricate a
measurement. All are post-race and are dropped regardless.

\emph{Systematic absence in a pre-race field.} \texttt{prize} is null for
462 races, or 7.3\,\% of the file, and the gap is not scattered: every
affected race falls between 20 July 1997 and 18 March 1998, a contiguous
block at the start of the record. This is missingness by collection
period, not at random, so mean or median imputation over the whole file
would import a late-period price level into early races. Because the split
is temporal, the entire block sits inside the training window, which
limits but does not remove the problem. \texttt{prize} is therefore kept
with an explicit ``unrecorded'' indicator, and imputation, where needed,
is performed within \texttt{race\_class} rather than globally, since prize
money is largely a function of class.

\emph{Sporadic absence.} \texttt{horse\_country} and \texttt{horse\_type}
are missing in two rows each and are encoded as an explicit
\texttt{Unknown} level. Dropping those rows is specifically rejected:
deleting a row removes a runner from a race, changing the size of the
field and breaking the one-winner-per-group structure the model depends
on. Mode imputation is rejected because it invents a country of origin the
model may then use. \texttt{place\_odds} is missing in 3\,735 rows, in a
way related to field size and pool availability rather than at random; the
column is excluded anyway, but mean imputation on a variable of that kind
would introduce bias rather than remove it.

\emph{Sentinels disguised as data.} Several columns use \texttt{999} to
encode ``not available'', which never appears as a null, so any mean or
correlation computed before cleaning is silently corrupted. The affected
fields are \texttt{lengths\_behind} (89 cells) and the sectional margin
and split-time columns (95 further cells). It is worth noting what is
\emph{not} a sentinel: \texttt{race\_id} and \texttt{horse\_id} legitimately
take the value 999, and 999\,lb is an ordinary \texttt{declared\_weight}
within the observed 693--1\,369\,lb range. A naive equality test flags all
three, which is why the check is applied only where 999 falls outside the
plausible range of the variable.

No univariate imputation is applied to derived historical features,
because absence there carries meaning: a debutant is not an average horse.
An indicator column is added instead, so a tree can split on it. Median
imputation is used only for physical measurements after sentinel removal,
and is fitted on training folds only.

\subsection{Outlier Detection}
\label{sec:outliers}

Beyond the sentinels above, three anomalies were found, and only one is an
outlier in the statistical sense.

\emph{Implausible physical values.} Three rows carry a
\texttt{declared\_weight} below 900\,lb and one below 800\,lb, which is
outside the plausible range for a racing thoroughbred and is treated as a
recording error. \texttt{actual\_weight} (103--133\,lb) and
\texttt{horse\_age} (2--10 years) fall within legitimate ranges and are
kept; the older horses are rare but real.

\emph{An impossible category value.} One row has \texttt{draw}~$=15$
although no field in the dataset exceeds 14 runners. With a single
occurrence this is a recording error rather than a pattern, but it is
reported because a model treating \texttt{draw} as an ordinal would
extrapolate from it.

\emph{Censoring.} \texttt{win\_odds} is capped at 99.0, and 9.4\,\% of
runners sit exactly at that ceiling. This is right-censoring, not an
outlier: the recorded value is an upper bound, so the ranking among
longest-priced runners is arbitrary and the market baseline must say so.

The treatment policy follows from the group structure: rows are never
deleted, because removing a runner alters the race it belongs to.
Anomalies are neutralised at the cell level --- the value becomes
\texttt{NaN} and a missing indicator is added --- so each race keeps its
full field.

\subsection{Class Imbalance}
\label{sec:imbalance}

Fields range from 4 to 14 runners with a mean of 12.5 and a median of 12,
so positives account for 8.0\,\% of rows, bounded between 7.1\,\% and
25\,\% depending on field size. The one-winner-per-race invariant holds in
6\,336 races; the remaining 12 (0.19\,\%) record two winners. These are
dead heats, not recording errors. They are retained, since discarding them
would bias the sample toward decisive finishes, and the top-1 metric counts
a hit when the highest-ranked runner is any of the joint winners.

The imbalance is \emph{structural} rather than a sampling defect: it is a
property of the task, not a distortion to correct. That rules out the
usual remedies. Synthetic oversampling is rejected on three grounds --- a
synthetic runner belongs to no race, so it destroys the group structure
the evaluation depends on; it breaks the constraint that win probabilities
within a race sum to one; and interpolation is meaningless where
\texttt{draw}, \texttt{jockey\_id} and \texttt{horse\_gear} have no
midpoint. Random undersampling fails for the same reason: removing
competitors changes how hard the race was to win. Class weighting is
admissible for the pointwise variant but distorts the output
probabilities, so scores must be recalibrated on the validation split
before any log loss is reported. We prefer not to treat the problem as
imbalanced at all, but to model the race as a group and normalise scores
within it, making the base rate a consequence of field size rather than a
nuisance parameter.

The metric plan follows. Accuracy is meaningless: a model that never
predicts a winner scores 92.0\,\%. Primary metrics are race-level top-1
accuracy and mean reciprocal rank; calibration is measured by log loss and
Brier score; discrimination is reported as ROC-AUC within races. Two
reference points anchor the scale. Selecting at random within the field
yields 8.1\,\% top-1 accuracy, and ranking by shortest final odds yields
29.8\,\%, so the market is 3.7 times better than chance. A model failing
to beat the first is broken; the second is the market benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:baseline-lit}, and closing the gap to it using pre-race
information alone is the substantive question of this work.

\subsection{Distributions and Correlations}
\label{sec:distributions}

Field size is concentrated at the upper end --- a median of 12 against a
maximum of 14 --- which governs the base rate above and motivates
reporting metrics per race rather than per row. The barrier effect is
clear and monotone: runners drawn in stall~1 win 10.5\,\% of the time
against 6.0\,\% for stall~13, a factor of 1.7 across the range, with the
decline steady rather than concentrated at the extremes.

The effect is not uniform across conditions. Splitting by course, the
advantage of an inside draw (stalls 1--4) is roughly twice as large at
Happy Valley, where those runners win 11.0\,\% against 7.3\,\% for the
rest of the field, as at Sha Tin, where the same comparison is 8.9\,\%
against 7.2\,\%; this is consistent with the tighter Happy Valley
circuit. By distance, however, the effect is not monotone: the gap is
2.8 points below 1\,200\,m and 3.1 points above 1\,600\,m, but only
1.3 points in between. Treating \texttt{draw} as a plain ordinal, or
interacting it linearly with distance, cannot represent that shape, which
is the concrete argument for a draw $\times$ distance $\times$ venue
interaction with distance entered categorically.

Among the pre-race numeric
features the notable structure is the near-collinearity between
\texttt{horse\_rating} and \texttt{actual\_weight}, expected because
handicap weight is assigned from the official rating; harmless for
gradient-boosted trees, but it must be handled in the logistic-regression
baseline by dropping one term or by regularisation.
