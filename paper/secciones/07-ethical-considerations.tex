\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

Two features of this problem make an early ethical review necessary
rather than ceremonial. The application domain is a regulated gambling
market, and the underlying activity has contested animal-welfare
implications. Both risks are properties of the domain and cannot be
engineered away; what can be decided in advance is the scope of the
artefact and the claims made about it. This section states the risks we
identified before training and the specific limits adopted in response.
The AI usage disclosure covers the separate question of tooling,
and the Stage~3 report revisits these issues with the deployed agent in
view.

\subsection{Scope of the artefact}

The system estimates a win probability for each runner from information
published before a race, and ranks the field accordingly. It is a
retrospective study on historical records from 1997--2005. It does not
consume live odds, holds no connection to any betting operator, and
includes no staking, bankroll or bet-selection component. The objective
function is probabilistic accuracy, not return on investment: the model
is selected and reported on log loss, Brier score, top-1 accuracy and mean
reciprocal rank, and never on simulated profit. This is a methodological
choice as much as an ethical one --- optimising for return would make
results dependent on assumptions about the tote pool that the dataset does
not support --- but it is also the boundary that keeps the project an
exercise in probability estimation rather than a wagering tool.

\subsection{Gambling-related harm}

The most obvious risk is dual use. A calibrated win probability is
directly actionable in a betting market, and Hong Kong hosts one of the
world's largest per-capita betting turnovers, with problem gambling
recognised as a public-health concern there. Publishing an accurate model
lowers the cost of participating in that market for anyone who finds it.

We do not claim this risk is eliminated; the mitigations bound it. The
repository carries an explicit non-deployment statement and no interface
to any betting platform. No trained model weights are published in a form
intended for reuse as a tipping service. The Stage~3 agent is scoped to
explaining and comparing predictions --- describing which pre-race
attributes moved a given estimate, and how the model's ranking compares
with the market's --- and declines requests for staking advice, stake
sizing or bet recommendations, with that refusal treated as a functional
requirement to be tested rather than a disclaimer in a README. Where
results are compared against market prices, the comparison is framed as a
test of informational efficiency, following the standard treatment in that
literature, and not as evidence that a profitable strategy exists.

A residual risk remains and we state it plainly: any work that improves
the informational efficiency of a betting market is of some use to people
betting in it. Declaring the limit does not remove it.

\subsection{Animal welfare}

Thoroughbred racing carries documented welfare concerns, including
racing-related injury and the fate of horses after their careers end.
This project does not model, optimise or recommend anything at the level
of an individual animal: it makes no recommendation about how a horse
should be trained, campaigned or run, and it produces no incentive
directed at a trainer or owner. It reuses records of races that were run
two decades ago. That said, the work belongs to an ecosystem whose
economics rest on the racing industry, and we note the tension rather than
resolve it. Nothing in the analysis should be read as a position on
whether the sport ought to exist.

\subsection{Identifiable individuals}

Jockeys and trainers appear as pseudonymous integer identifiers, but the
pseudonymisation is weak: because the underlying race records are public,
an identifier can be re-linked to a named professional by matching dates,
venues and results. The features we build include jockey and trainer strike
rates, so the model assigns what amounts to a performance score to real
people whose livelihood depends on their reputation.

Three limits follow. Identifiers stay pseudonymous in every published
output, including figures and appendices. No ranking of individual jockeys
or trainers is produced or published, even though the intermediate
features would permit one. Feature-importance analysis is reported at the
level of feature families --- ``jockey history'', ``barrier'', ``weight''
--- rather than for specific individuals. Horse identity raises no
comparable concern, and \texttt{horse\_id} is used without restriction.

\subsection{Representativeness and the limits of the claim}

The data come from two courses under a single regulator, within a fixed
historical window, and under Hong Kong's particular handicap and class
system. Results generalise to that setting and no further; presenting them
as a general statement about racing would be an overclaim. There is also
drift within the window itself --- rule changes, track work, and turnover
among jockeys and trainers --- which is one reason the evaluation uses a
temporal split (Sec.~\ref{sec:leakage}): it makes degradation over time
visible instead of averaging it away, at the cost of a harder test set.
Any bias present in the historical records, including systematic
differences in how horses of different origins or classes were rated and
handicapped, is reproduced by a model fitted to them.

\subsection{Calibration and overclaiming}

A win probability presented without its uncertainty invites misuse, and
the temptation in this domain is to read a poorly calibrated score as a
statement of fact. Racing outcomes carry substantial irreducible
uncertainty: the best achievable performance is far from perfect
prediction, and a model reporting high confidence is more likely
miscalibrated than skilled. We therefore report calibration explicitly
through log loss, Brier score and reliability diagrams alongside ranking
metrics, and state the irreducible-uncertainty limit in the abstract and
the conclusions rather than only in an appendix.
